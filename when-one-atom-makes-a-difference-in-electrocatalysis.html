When One Atom Makes a Difference in Electrocatalysis

How atomically defined metal clusters help connect catalyst size, structure, support interactions, and electrochemical performance.

By Lokesh Saravanan October 2026

When considering a metal catalyst such as platinum, it is easy to assume that making particles smaller simply increases surface area and therefore improves catalytic activity. At the nanoscale, however, the situation becomes more complex. Once a catalyst is reduced to only a few atoms, changing the cluster by even one atom can alter its geometry, electronic structure, adsorption behavior, and interaction with the support.

This is where size-selected clusters become especially valuable.

From nanoparticles to atomically defined clusters

Conventional nanoparticles usually contain a distribution of particle sizes and structures. When many different structures are present in the same catalyst, it becomes difficult to determine which specific atomic arrangement is responsible for the observed activity.

Size-selected cluster deposition provides a more controlled approach. Clusters containing a defined number of atoms such as Pt2, Pt4 or Pt7 can be generated, mass-selected, and deposited onto an electrode surface. This creates a well-defined starting point that allows catalytic behavior to be examined at nearly the atomic level.

Why can one atom make a difference?

Atoms in bulk metals are surrounded by many neighboring atoms. In clusters containing only a few atoms, most atoms are undercoordinated, meaning they have fewer neighboring metal atoms than they would in a bulk material.

This changes how they interact with reaction intermediates.

Small clusters can also be fluxional, meaning their atomic structures can rearrange under reaction conditions rather than behaving like rigid miniature versions of bulk metals. These structural changes can affect how strongly species such as hydrogen bind to the catalyst and how easily electrochemical reactions proceed.

At this scale, the relationship can be thought of as:

cluster size → atomic structure → adsorption behavior → catalytic activity

Size-selected clusters make it possible to explore this connection systematically.

Size is important, but it is not the whole story

Cluster size alone does not determine catalytic performance. The support material and deposition conditions can strongly influence the final structure of the catalyst.

For example, Pt clusters deposited gently onto carbon surfaces can interact weakly with the support, allowing them to diffuse and aggregate into larger structures. At higher deposition energies, the impact can create defects in the carbon lattice, break Pt–Pt and C–C bonds, and form strongly bound Pt–C structures.

As a result, two clusters that initially contain the same number of Pt atoms may develop very different catalytic structures depending on how they interact with the surface.

Small, strongly anchored Pt–C structures can provide highly active sites, while Pt atoms buried too deeply below the surface may become inaccessible to the electrolyte and contribute little to the reaction.

This highlights an important point: cluster size matters, but support interactions, aggregation, and accessibility of active sites can be equally important.

Connecting atomic structure with catalytic performance

Size-selected clusters provide a useful bridge between experiment and theory.

Electrochemical measurements can determine how well a catalyst performs, while techniques such as X-ray photoelectron spectroscopy, ion scattering, and electron microscopy can reveal changes in composition and structure. At the same time, molecular dynamics and density functional theory can model realistic atomic structures and reaction pathways.

Together, these approaches allow researchers to move beyond simply asking:

“Which material gives the higher activity?”

Instead, they can ask:

“Which atomic structure is responsible for the activity, and why?”

The bigger picture

The importance of size-selected clusters is not simply that smaller is better. Their real value is that they provide a controlled platform for understanding catalysis one atom at a time.

By controlling cluster size, deposition conditions, and support chemistry, researchers can begin to separate the effects of atomic coordination, electronic structure, catalyst–support interactions, and active-site accessibility.

In this way, size-selected clusters serve as powerful model systems for identifying the atomic-scale design principles that could guide the development of more efficient electrocatalysts.
